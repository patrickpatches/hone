Smash Burger

The crispy-edged diner classic

Before you start

A heavy steel or cast-iron pan is not optional. Non-stick can't reach the heat — it warps and releases fumes. Cast iron, carbon steel, or stainless works; just give it longer to heat.

Get your mise en place ready before the pan goes on. Smashing happens fast — you have about 10 seconds once the beef hits the heat. Portion the balls, spatula close, cheese sliced and waiting.

80/20 mince is the move. Leaner mince makes a dry burger. The fat renders and builds the crispy, lacy edge — that's what makes a smash burger a smash burger.

01
Mise en place
5 min

Divide your mince into 125 g balls per patty — don't pack them tight, loose is better. Dice the onion very finely, slice the pickles, and get the cheese out so it's at room temperature. Set everything within arm's reach of the stove before you light the burner.

WhyOnce the beef hits the pan you have 10 seconds to smash it before it steams instead of sears. There's no time to go looking for anything.
02
Heat the pan
5 min

Put the pan over high heat. Wait until it's just starting to smoke — 4 to 5 minutes on gas, longer on electric. Add a teaspoon of neutral oil and swirl; it should shimmer straight away.

Stage photo
WhyThe crust — the Maillard reaction — needs surface temps above 150°C. A lukewarm pan steams the beef grey; a screaming-hot pan sears it brown.
03
Smash & sear
2 min

Drop a ball of mince in the pan. Press it down hard straight away with the spatula — lean your weight in — and hold for 10 seconds. Season generously with sea salt. Don't touch it for 90 seconds. Lift when the edges are deeply browned and lacy.

WhyThe smash maximises contact with the pan, and more contact means more crust. The lacy edge is the whole point — that's where the flavour lives.
04
Cheese & steam
45 sec

Flip the patty and lay two slices of cheese across the top straight away. Add a splash of water to the pan and cover with a lid or foil for 30–45 seconds. Lift the lid — the cheese should be melted and glossy.

WhyAmerican cheese melts flawlessly because it's engineered to. The steam pocket finishes the melt without overcooking the beef. If you swapped to cheddar, give it an extra 15 seconds.
05
Toast the bun
1 min

In the same pan (don't wipe it — that fond is flavour), drop to medium heat. Press the cut sides of the bun face-down for 45 to 60 seconds until golden. Watch it — brioche burns fast.

WhyA toasted bun is structural as much as it is flavour — it won't sog out under the sauce and juices the way a raw bun does.
06
Build & serve

Bottom bun → sauce → diced onion → lettuce → patty (cheese up) → pickles → sauce on the top bun → close. Serve straight away — a smash burger is a 90-second window.

WhyOrder matters: onion under the patty catches the residual heat and softens slightly; pickles on top stay crisp. Sauce on both sides is non-negotiable.
Design rationale — v4 "The Pass"

v3 was a good skeleton and I kept its bones: one rust "Start Cooking" CTA with the other actions demoted to ghost links, the single gold section-header language (label + chevron on every collapsible block), equipment before ingredients, the step preview carousel feeding the full method, and a why-note on every step. Those decisions were right. v4 fixes four places where v3 was either quiet, incomplete, or only coincidentally working because the example was a burger.

What changed, and why

1 · The "why" note is no longer the quietest thing on the step
v3 set the why-note in muted grey — literally the lowest-contrast text on the page — while CLAUDE.md says "explain the underlying reason, always." That's the product's whole personality whispering. v4 lifts it to ink-soft on a gold-dim panel with a full-strength gold marker and a solid gold left rule. It's still calm and still Playfair italic, but it now reads as a head chef leaning in, not a footnote. Confidence comes from understanding why — a cook who knows why can recover when something drifts.
Engineer cost: CSS only — trivial
2 · "At a glance" tells a story instead of five equal chips
v3 gave time, difficulty, cuisine and "no leftovers" the same visual weight, so the 2-second scan had to read all of them to find the two that decide "can I make this tonight." v4 leads with the two decision-drivers — time and effort, weighted and labelled ("start to plate", "fast & forgiving") — then drops yield and cuisine to a quieter right-hand column. Crucially it now respects active vs total time: when they diverge (a braise: 25 min active, 2 hr total) it shows both, because that gap is the single most useful fact for planning your evening. The burger's are the same, so it shows one.
Engineer cost: reads existing total_time_minutes / active_time_minutes — small
3 · An allergen & dietary strip — the "before you commit" signal
The one thing a head chef tells you before you start that v3 never captured. A neutral, honest strip directly under the glance row: what the dish contains (gluten, dairy, egg…) and a plain dietary marker ("Not vegetarian"). Kept deliberately calm — informational, not alarm-coloured — so it informs without shouting. This is the only genuinely new data the page needs; see the engineer note.
Engineer cost: needs new allergens[] / dietary fields on the Recipe schema — flagged, not faked
4 · Equipment defaults collapsed — but names the blockers while closed
v3 defaulted Equipment open, which puts a 4-row list between every repeat cook and the recipe. But a first-timer genuinely needs to know they require a cast-iron pan before they shop. v4 collapses it by default and puts the essentials in the closed summary — "Needs: cast-iron pan, flat spatula · +2 more" — so the blocking information is visible without a tap, and the detail (notes, optional items, Essential/Optional badges) is one tap away. First-timers open it; everyone else scrolls past.
Engineer cost: collapse default + summary line — small (but see equipment schema note)
5 · The leftover nudge has a home (it was missing entirely in v3)
The schema carries leftover_mode + extra_for_tomorrow_label, and v3 dropped it. v4 gives the scaling section a nudge slot directly under the stepper — a gold-dim "Make extra for tomorrow?" tap-target — rendered only when leftover_mode !== 'none'. The burger is a serve-now dish so it's hidden here; the braise demo below shows it on.
Engineer cost: conditional render of existing fields — small

Why this holds for every recipe — now and future

The page is built from recipe fields, never burger assumptions. The hero pulls hero_url (with a designed fallback when it's absent — see below), the stepper label is output_unit / output_unit_plural ("burgers", "loaves", "cups", "serves"), the glance row reads total/active_time_minutes, and Equipment, Before-you-start, swaps, timers, why-notes and step photos are all per-recipe data with graceful empty states. Honest note for Patrick

I did not keep v3 just because it was already approved-adjacent. Two of these changes — the louder why-note and the allergen strip — are the ones I'd defend hardest: the first because the current treatment quietly contradicts the one rule that makes Hone Hone, and the second because "what a chef tells you before you commit" is exactly the gap you pointed at. The allergen strip is the only change that needs new recipe data; everything else reads fields that already exist. If you'd rather ship the layout first and add allergens in a follow-up, the strip drops out cleanly and the rest stands on its own.
